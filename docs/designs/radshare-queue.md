## Problem Statement

Radsharing in Warframe requires four players holding the same Void Relic at the same
refinement to open it together. Coordinating that today means in-game recruiting chat —
a wall of text that scrolls your message out of view in seconds — or a Discord server,
where a message posted at 14:00 is answered at 15:30, long after the moment passed.

The gap this closes is the moment of assembly: four people, same relic, same refinement,
online right now. Everything else in the space is a board you post to and walk away from.

**The wedge is rare relics, not common ones.** For a common relic, recruiting chat works —
post, wait a minute, fill the squad. That is why every general-purpose LFG board in this space
looks redundant and reads `0/0`: it competes with something that already works. The case that
genuinely cannot be served in-game is the long tail. For a vaulted or uncommon relic, four
holders are essentially never in region chat at the same moment, so the squad never forms at
all — not slowly, never. This is first-hand user observation, not inference.

That narrows the product and changes what it must be good at:

- **A rare-relic squad takes longer to form than a common one, and how much longer is unknown.**
  `1/4` — just you — is the normal state for a rare bucket, not a failure state. The design has
  to make waiting *tolerable*, which is a different problem from making an empty board look
  alive. Earlier drafts asserted "an hour"; that number was invented during review and is not
  evidence. Nothing in the design should depend on a specific wait length.
- **A fill-descending global list buries exactly the buckets this exists for.** One person
  waiting on a vaulted Lith sits below forty common buckets at 2/4. This is why the board goes
  personal the moment you queue (see *The board*): the rare-relic user names their relic and
  watches it, instead of scanning a leaderboard of commons.
- **Common relics are still welcome**, and they are what makes the board look populated in a
  screenshot. They are the shop window; rare relics are the reason to come back.

## What Makes This Cool

**The fill counter is the product.** `Axi G9 · Radiant · 3/4` is not a status indicator,
it is an invitation. An opaque "searching…" spinner reads dead even when it is working.
A board of near-complete squads pulls people in, and joining is the act that completes one.

Two consequences fall out of that:

1. **The board is the proof surface, not the launch artifact.** A live board with buckets at
   3/4 is genuinely compelling, and a room-listing competitor structurally cannot produce that
   image, because a listing is the residue of someone who already left. But on day one almost
   every row reads `1/4` — honest, normal for a rare relic, and it reads to a stranger as
   nobody-is-here. **The launch artifact is a clip of the flow instead** (see *Distribution
   Plan*). The board earns the screenshot later, once it is busy on its own.
2. **The empty state is the launch state, and it has to be designed, not defaulted.**
   Being first in a bucket is fine. Being first in an apparent void is not. Those are the
   same state with different information. The activity signal is what makes a sparse board
   read as early rather than dead.

## Constraints

- **Web only.** No desktop client. No reading of local Warframe log files, ever.
- **No Discord dependency.** No bot, no server-as-the-product, no Discord in the UI chrome.
  Discord OAuth as a login provider is fine — the constraint is on Discord as the product
  surface, not as an identity provider.
- **The squad forms in-game.** The app hands the host three in-game names to whisper, with a
  copy-whisper button on desktop and tablet. It cannot put anyone in a squad; the last mile is
  always manual.
- **No player inventory exists.** Digital Extremes exposes no API for what relics a player
  holds. Relic ownership is claimed, never verified.
- **Don't overcomplicate.** Stated directly and applied repeatedly through the session.

## Premises

1. **The last mile is always an in-game invite.** The host sends three whispers — copied on
   desktop, typed on a phone.
2. **Presence, not posting.** Queue entries are heartbeat-backed and die when the account's
   **last** socket closes — clients are independent, so an account may hold several at once.
   **Minimizing the tab must not evict you**, which constrains the heartbeat design (see
   *Presence*): liveness uses protocol-level ping frames, a clean close evicts at once, and an
   abnormal close holds the entry 60 seconds. A user already matched into a lobby can reconnect
   to it; queue entry is ephemeral, lobby membership is durable.
3. **Liquidity is the main risk, not features.** v1 spends its budget on multi-select, on
   making waiting cheap (background tab, audible ping, title flip), and on the activity signal.
4. **A queue pop must be confirmed.** All four press Ready within 60 seconds or the match is
   void. Whoever did not ready is removed from the queue; the others go back into it. The lobby
   only exists once four people have confirmed.
5. **Nothing verifies relic ownership, and nothing scores users.** There is no reputation
   system. The ready gate is the product's only enforcement, and it enforces *attendance*, not
   honesty — someone can still show up with the wrong refinement. The gate is chosen over
   reputation because it acts immediately, applies automatically, and works at zero volume,
   which a score never does.
6. **A matched squad gets a chatroom.** Explicit product requirement, not an optional extra.
   The whisper gets people into a squad; the chatroom is where they coordinate which relic and
   confirm refinement before loading in.
7. **Game data is a dependency, not a scrape** — WFCD's `warframe-items` /
   `warframe-relic-data` / `warframe-drop-data`, vendored as build-time JSON.
8. **Distribution has no app store.** Launch means r/Warframe, the forums, and Warframe
   Discords. Marketing there is fine; depending on it is not.
9. **The target case is the rare relic.** Common-relic squads already form in recruiting chat;
   rare-relic squads do not form in-game at all. Where a design choice trades common-relic
   speed against rare-relic reachability, rare-relic reachability wins.

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: Single Process — CHOSEN

One Bun + Hono server holds queue state in memory and talks WebSocket to a SvelteKit client.
SQLite on the machine's volume holds all durable state. Effort M, risk Low.

### Approach B: Nakama-backed — rejected

Self-hosted Heroic Labs Nakama for auth, sockets, presence, chat and leaderboards. Rejected:
learning a Go game server for a side project, and its matchmaker is opaque by design — it
cannot expose per-bucket counts, which is the one thing that is actually the product.

### Approach C: Wave-first — rejected

Hourly scheduled matching with a countdown landing page. Originated in the cold read, not the
user. Rejected: users queue whenever they want.

## Recommended Approach

### Stack and topology

TypeScript end to end. **One** Bun process runs Hono, which serves the SvelteKit client *and*
terminates the WebSocket on the same origin — no CORS, no second runtime.

**The client is not purely prerendered.** The board route is server-rendered so the cached board
payload lands in the HTML on first paint, and `/lobby/[id]`, `/join` and `/profile` are dynamic
routes that cannot be prerendered at all. Expect SSR for the board and a fallback for the rest,
not `adapter-static` with a blanket prerender.

Monorepo: `apps/server`, `apps/web`, `packages/protocol`. **SQLite for durable state**, via the
built-in `bun:sqlite` — a file on the Fly volume, no connection pool, no second service, no
network hop. The design already pins everything to one machine because the queue lives in that
process's memory, so Postgres would have been paying for multi-writer capability the
architecture forbids. Backups are **litestream**, streaming the file to object storage; that is
the one thing this choice makes yours to own.
Relic data vendored from WFCD as build-time JSON; `relicId` is the WFCD `uniqueName` string,
carried verbatim so the vendored file is the only source of truth.

### Queue state and identity

A `Map<BucketKey, Bucket>`. `BucketKey` is `` `${relicId}:${refinement}` ``. `refinement` is
the enum `intact | exceptional | flawless | radiant`. Platform is **not** in the key.

A `Bucket` holds an **ordered list** of `{ accountId, enqueuedAt }`, ascending by `enqueuedAt`.
Entries are keyed by **account ID, never by socket**: one account occupies at most one slot per
bucket, which is what prevents manufacturing a fake 4/4 from four tabs.

**Every client is independent, and an account may hold many sockets at once.** Desktop, phone
and a second tab are separate connections that each see the same account state; none of them
supersedes or disconnects another. The server keeps a socket set per account, and **eviction
happens when the account's last socket closes**, not when any one of them does. An earlier draft
had a second socket take over from the first with a `session.superseded` takeover screen; that
is removed, along with the screen, the reclaim button and the message.

Two things fall out of this. Locking a phone while the desktop is still open does not touch your
queue entry, which matters because iOS suspends a backgrounded or locked app and a WebSocket is
not a connection type a suspended app may keep — the socket closes within seconds regardless of
protocol pings. And the match gong plays on every open client, so whichever one you are near is
the one that reaches you.

**Buckets are created on demand and deleted when empty.** A `Map` entry exists only while at
least one account is in it: the first joiner creates it, and removing the last member deletes
the key. There is no pre-created set of buckets, and there never needs to be — roughly 500
relics times 4 refinements would be 2,000 permanently empty entries representing nobody.

**Therefore `0/4` is not a state this system can be in**, and the board never renders it. Your
own bucket is at minimum `1/4`, because you are in it. Earlier drafts of this document described
`0/4` as the normal state for a rare relic and the mockups render `0/4` rows; both are wrong and
came from mockup artifacts rather than from the data model. The lone-member state is `1/4` and
reads "You're first in line for Axi G9".

Nothing about the queue is persisted server-side.

**Cap: 20 `(relic, refinement)` pairs per account**, enforced server-side on `queue.join`. The
cap is on the product, not the relic list — 10 relics × 4 refinements would be 40 buckets, and
at launch scale one user's 40 single-person buckets would be a visible fraction of the board,
which is the exact inflation the cap exists to prevent.

### Matching semantics

1. A join is **one atomic operation**: the user's whole selection is applied in a single pass
   with no broadcast partway through.
2. Within that pass, **at most one bucket may fire**, even if several reach four.
3. Tiebreak is the **oldest-waiting bucket**, by the `enqueuedAt` of its oldest current entry;
   then `BucketKey` lexicographic order. Deterministic and testable.
4. Selection within a bucket is **FIFO by `enqueuedAt`** — an explicit contract, not an
   accident of insertion order.
5. On fire: the four members are removed from **every** bucket they occupy, deltas are
   computed, and only then is a single broadcast emitted. Eviction completes before broadcast.
6. Consequently `4/4` is never observable, and no bucket can be stranded at four by a join —
   every bucket that reached four contains the joiner, and the joiner is evicted. The two
   rendering invariants are therefore **never `0/4`** (empty buckets are deleted, not shown)
   and **never `4/4`** (a full bucket fires and disappears in the same synchronous pass).
7. Buckets dropping from 3/4 to 2/4 because their members were taken emit a normal count
   update. No special notification.

**Atomicity is a property of the code, not the runtime.** Single-threaded JavaScript guarantees
atomicity only between `await` points, and a database write is an await.

(`bun:sqlite` is synchronous, so in practice the insert may not yield at all — but the invariant
must not depend on that. Litestream, a future migration, or a switch to an async driver would
silently break a rule that leaned on it. The pure-function boundary holds regardless of driver.) The rule is therefore
enforced **structurally, not by convention**:

```
  planMatch(buckets, request, now) -> PlanResult    PURE. No I/O reachable.
    |                                               Nothing to await because
    |  idempotent set join                          nothing external is in scope.
    |  at most one bucket fires
    |  evict the four from EVERY bucket
    |  compute deltas
    v
  Plan { deltas[], readyCheck | null, evicted[] }
    |
    +--> caller performs ALL I/O:
         open the ready check, await confirmations, then insert the lobby
            |
            +-- all confirm, insert ok --> broadcast, send match.found
            |
            +-- gate failed or insert failed -->
                  planRestore(buckets, evicted.filter(shouldComeBack))
                  restores ORIGINAL enqueuedAt and rebroadcasts

  planRestore(buckets, placements) -> Plan          also pure, also may fire
  planLeave(buckets, accountId)    -> Plan
```

**`planRestore` is a function, not an `undo()` closure on the plan, and that is
a correction from implementation.** A closure captures the world as it was at
fire time and would run up to a minute later, after other people have joined
those buckets; restoring blindly against a changed map can push a bucket past
four. Re-evaluating on restore means a restore that completes a bucket fires it,
which is right — those are four real people waiting on the same relic. It also
makes the lobby-insert retry fall out for free: put the same four back and they
re-fire immediately, which is why the caller backs off after repeated
`MATCH_FAILED` rather than looping.

`planMatch(map, request, now)` returns a plain object. It touches nothing external, so there is
nothing to await. A test asserts its return is not a `Promise`, which fails the moment someone
breaks the rule.

**Time and connection identity are injected, never reached for.** `now` is a parameter, and the
bucket layer stores an opaque `connectionId` rather than a socket object; socket handlers are a
thin shell that calls into it. Almost everything that makes the matcher correct is about
ordering in time — who queued first, which bucket has waited longest, whether an undo preserves
a place in line — and a direct `Date.now()` turns each of those tests into queue-sleep-hope,
which is slow, flaky on CI, and skipped within a month. With `now` handed in, every ordering
test is exact and instant. With connection identity opaque, eviction-on-disconnect is tested by
calling a function instead of tearing down a real socket.

**The undo is why eviction can safely precede persistence.** Eviction is synchronous and
in-memory; the insert can still fail on a disk error, an OOM, or a deploy landing in that
window. Without compensation the four are removed from every queue with no lobby to show
for it, and nothing on their screen says so — `match.found` never fires. `MatchPlan.undo()`
restores them to their original buckets with their **original `enqueuedAt`**, so they keep their
place in line. The failure becomes a visible, self-healing hiccup instead of a silent hole.

The point of restoring `enqueuedAt` is honesty, not competitive advantage. There is no room
scarcity — any four people in a bucket match — so a lost timestamp usually costs nothing at all.
It costs something only when others joined that bucket during the gap, which on a rare relic is
rare. Restore it because silently moving someone is wrong, not because the position is valuable.

Known limit of the undo: if writes are failing persistently rather than transiently — a full or
failing volume — a restored user can immediately re-match and fail again. Back off after repeated
`MATCH_FAILED` for the same bucket rather than looping.

Because entries only ever arrive via `queue.join` and a join evicts its own joiner on fire, a
bucket can never exceed four. Rule 4's ordering is stated as a contract for determinism, and a
bucket size greater than four is an assertion failure, not a case to handle.

### The board

Bucket size is the fill counter. **The board has two modes, and the mode is a function of queue
state, not of authentication.** It is one surface that swaps contents, not two pages and not one
view with a filter on it.

**Content follows queue state. Transport follows authentication.** Two axes, not one.

| | Global board | Your own buckets |
|---|---|---|
| **Anonymous** | Cached `GET /api/board`, 10s cache, polled every 10s, server-rendered into first paint | n/a — cannot queue |
| **Signed in** | Live over the socket | Live over the socket |

**The global board** is every non-empty bucket, fill descending then bucket age, capped at the
top 60. It is the proof surface — it shows a stranger that real people are waiting right now, it
is the view you screenshot for r/Warframe, and it is where a signed-in player opportunistically
notices that `Axi A2` sits at 3/4 when they happen to hold that relic. That last part is why
**signed-in users get it live**: watching a bucket reach 3/4 is what prompts someone to join it,
and a ten-second delay blunts the one mechanism that completes other people's squads.

**Anonymous viewers get the same board on a 10-second cache instead of a socket.** They are
reading, not participating, and the difference buys a lot:

- **A hundred anonymous readers cost one cached query, not a hundred live connections.** The
  single pinned machine survives its own launch post, and the endpoint can move to an edge cache
  without touching the app.
- **The same payload is rendered into the HTML on first paint**, so a visitor from Reddit sees a
  populated board before JavaScript runs and the link preview is not blank. The board can only be
  the marketing asset if it is there before the page boots.
- **The cheapest flood stops being socket state.** What remains reachable by socket is an
  authenticated account, which is a far narrower surface.

A ten-second board is still honest: the row carries `updated Ns ago`.

**Queued — your board.** The moment you hit Queue the socket's contents swap to your own buckets.
It answers one question — "is my thing happening?" — with no noise from forty commons filling
above it. Leave the queue and it swaps back to global.

**If the join instantly completes a bucket to four, the personal board is never seen at all:**
`match.found` fires in the same pass and the user routes straight to the lobby.

Tying the mode to queue state rather than auth state is what keeps both properties. Nobody ever
faces an empty board, because an unqueued user always has the global one. And the rare-relic user
of premise 9 — who arrives knowing exactly which vaulted relic they want and would otherwise be
scanning a list of commons — gets a view of just their own the instant they commit.

This removes the lenses, and it removes the `myBuckets` strip pinned above a public list, because
while queued your buckets **are** the board.

It does **not** remove the two-stream subscription switch. A signed-in socket carries the global
stream while unqueued and the own-buckets stream once queued, so `queue.join` and `queue.leave`
still swap what that socket is subscribed to, and `inBoard` and `hiddenCount` are still needed on
the global stream to tell the client that a bucket entered or left the top 60. An earlier draft
of this section claimed those were deleted; that was only true under the assumption that nobody
received the global board live, which is not the design.

The free-text relic filter moves to the composer, where it belongs — it is how you find the relic
to add, not how you scan a list.

**The swap itself is a state change worth designing.** It is the visible confirmation that
queueing worked, and it is the only feedback the user gets when a match does not fire immediately.
Cross-fade, 120ms, with the section label changing from `LIVE QUEUE BOARD` to `YOUR QUEUE`.

### Presence

**Only authenticated accounts hold sockets.** An anonymous visitor polls the cached board and is
never a connection. A signed-in user holds a socket whether or not they are queued, because live
global updates are what prompt them to join someone else's near-full bucket.

For a queued user the socket is the queue entry. Clean disconnect removes the account from every
bucket immediately.

**Verified locally on Bun 1.4.2 (`T-spike`, local half).** An idle connection that sent zero
application messages survived 20s against an 8s `idleTimeout` — the open Bun issue about
`idleTimeout` fighting `sendPings` did not reproduce. Close codes distinguish the two cases the
grace rule needs: **1000/1001 clean, 1006 abnormal**. A peer process killed outright fired
`close` in **1ms**.

**Also verified through a real Caddy 2.11.4 reverse proxy.** WebSocket upgrade needs no special
configuration. Pongs arrived on exactly the same cadence as a direct connection, an idle socket
survived 20s against an 8s `idleTimeout`, and an abruptly killed peer still produced **1006**
through the proxy. Nothing about the presence design changes behind Caddy.

**Liveness uses WebSocket protocol-level ping frames, not an application message.** The browser
answers ping frames in its network stack with no JavaScript involved, so a throttled or frozen
background tab stays connected. An application-level `pong` would be subject to Chrome's
background-tab timer throttling (roughly one run per minute) and would evict exactly the
minimized-tab users premises 2 and 3 exist to protect.

**You do not configure a ping interval — you configure `idleTimeout`, and Bun pings at half of
it.** Measured: an 8s `idleTimeout` produced pongs at 4s, 8s, 12s and 16s. **`idleTimeout: 30`**
therefore pings roughly every 15s and gives up after 30s of silence. **There is no sweep and no
`lastPongAt` bookkeeping to write**: Bun evicts on timeout and fires `close`, and eviction from
buckets happens there.

Two staleness bounds, because they are different failures. A peer that **dies** sends FIN and
`close` fires within about a millisecond — measured at 1ms for a killed process, and unchanged
through the proxy. A peer that **vanishes silently** (network partition, no FIN) is invisible
until `idleTimeout`, so the board can be stale for up to 30 seconds. With the grace window
removed, that is the whole worst case rather than the first half of it.

**Any close evicts immediately. There is no grace window.** A deliberate tab close and a dead
router are treated identically, because the board must not vouch for someone who is not
connected — that is the whole of premise 2.

An earlier design held an account's entries for 60 seconds after an abnormal close, so a brief
blip did not cost a queue position. **It was removed during implementation**, for three reasons
that compound:

- **The position it protected is worth almost nothing.** There is no room scarcity; any four
  people in a bucket match. Losing `enqueuedAt` costs only whoever joined that bucket in the
  meantime, which on a rare relic is usually nobody.
- **The client already recovers a blip.** It caches the relic selection and re-queues on
  reconnect — machinery that exists for deploys regardless. Grace was a second mechanism doing
  the same job.
- **It tripled the ghost window.** A *silent* death costs `idleTimeout` before the server even
  notices; stacking 60 seconds of grace on top meant the board could count a departed player for
  90+ seconds. Long enough for three real people to fill that bucket, fire a ready check
  containing a ghost, and lose a minute when it cannot complete.

Close codes are still distinguishable and verified — `ws.close()` reports 1000, `ws.close(1001)`
reports 1001, a killed peer reports 1006, and all of it survives the reverse proxy. The design
simply no longer needs to branch on them. Note this is per account, not per socket: with independent clients, the hold
only starts when the account's last socket goes.

**Wake lock is a mobile concern and is deferred with the rest of phone support** (see
*Responsive*). Recorded here so the reasoning is not lost: iOS suspends a backgrounded or locked
app and a WebSocket is not a connection type a suspended app may keep, so a phone client's socket
dies within seconds of the screen sleeping — regardless of protocol pings. The Screen Wake Lock
API, supported in Safari 16.4+ and now every browser, holds the screen on while queued and
prevents that. It must be requested from the QUEUE gesture and re-requested on `visibilitychange`,
because a wake lock is auto-released whenever the page is hidden. It prevents automatic sleep,
not a deliberate power-button press.

### Abuse limits

The anonymous surface is a cached `GET /api/board`, so the cheapest flood is absorbed by the
cache rather than by socket state, and it can be moved to an edge cache without touching the app.
Sockets are reachable only by a signed-in account that is queueing, which narrows the surface
considerably. What remains still needs limits, because the deployment is deliberately a single
pinned machine.

- **5 concurrent sockets per IP**, rejected at the **upgrade handshake** rather than after, so a
  connection flood never allocates socket state. Only authenticated accounts reach this path.
  **The IP must come from `X-Forwarded-For`, never the socket peer.** Verified: behind Caddy the
  app sees `::ffff:127.0.0.1`, and the peer port was *identical across separate requests* because
  Caddy pools its upstream connection. So the peer is not merely always-loopback, it is a single
  connection **shared between different visitors** — keying a per-IP cap on it would count the
  entire userbase as one person and lock everyone out at the sixth connection.

  **Caddy replaces `X-Forwarded-For` rather than appending to it**, verified by forging both a
  single value and a chain: both were discarded and the real peer came through. So reading the
  header directly is safe *by default*. It stops being safe the moment someone adds
  `trusted_proxies` to the Caddyfile, which makes Caddy append and trust what the client sent.
  Do not add it without re-testing this.

  Normalise the address before use: the peer arrives IPv6-mapped (`::ffff:127.0.0.1`).
- **Token bucket of ~20 messages per 10 seconds per socket.** Over-limit messages are dropped
  with an `error`, not silently.
- **Share-code attempts rate-limited per IP**, separately and more tightly, since that is the
  one endpoint where guessing has a payoff.

In-process, no new dependency, invisible to anyone who opens one tab. Per-IP is blunt behind
shared NAT — a few users in the same building could collide at 5 sockets — which is an accepted
tradeoff at this scale and the number to raise first if real users hit it.

### Lobby lifecycle

Durable. **Lobbies hold no in-memory state beyond the socket registry.** Ghost determination and
dissolution are computed from `created_at` / `last_seen_at` by a periodic sweep and on read —
never by `setTimeout`, which would be lost on every deploy and leave post-deploy lobbies that
never dissolve.

- **One member is the designated host, and the four screens are not identical.** The host is
  the member with the oldest `enqueuedAt` — already tracked by the matcher, deterministic, no
  new state, and it quietly rewards the longest wait, which matters most on a rare relic. The
  server assigns it; it is never negotiated in chat.
  - **The host sees:** "You're the host. Invite these three." plus the three in-game names, each
    with a **copy-whisper button** that copies `/w <IGN> radshare Axi G9 Radiant` to the
    clipboard. Only the host gets these buttons — the other three have nobody to invite, and
    four people each whispering three others is twelve whispers and colliding invites.
  - **Everyone else sees:** "<HostIGN> will invite you. Keep Warframe open."
  - **Copy-whisper buttons are desktop-and-tablet only (≥601px).** On a phone the clipboard
    cannot reach Warframe, because the game is not on that device — so below 601px the buttons
    are absent and the names are read and typed. IGNs render large, in mono, and selectable at
    every width, with the whisper format shown as a reference line beneath the list.
  - **Warframe in-game names are plain — there is no `#1234` discriminator.** Earlier mockups
    invented one and it briefly reached this document. Any mockup showing `zylok#314` is wrong;
    the name is `zylok`.
  - **No host handoff.** If the host goes AFK, members use **Queue again** and go back to the
    queue. A live role transfer was considered and rejected as overcomplication.
- **Queue again is always available to every member**, not only after someone leaves. An AFK
  host never presses Leave, so a button scoped to the post-leave case would never appear in the
  exact situation that needs it.
- **Contents:** all four members listed, with the viewer's own row marked **YOU**. Each row
  shows IGN, platform and self-declared mastery rank if set. Every member in a lobby has already
  confirmed at the ready gate, so there is no readiness column here and no reputation column —
  reputation does not exist in this product. Plus chat.
  **Mastery rank is self-declared in the profile**, exactly like the IGN, because DE exposes no
  player API. It is a courtesy signal, never a gate, and absent when unset.
- **There is no READY button in the lobby.** An earlier draft had one per member, with the host
  watching badges fill live — "informs the host, never blocks them". The **ready gate replaced it
  entirely**: readiness is now settled *before* the lobby exists, so a badge here would be a
  second and weaker copy of a question already answered. The host whispers on arrival, with
  nothing to wait for and nothing to watch.
- **Everyone here already confirmed.** A lobby only exists once all four passed the ready gate,
  so no-shows are filtered out before this screen and there is no readiness state inside it.
- **The intended happy path is that you leave.** You read the names, alt-tab into Warframe, and
  abandon the browser. This must never be punished.
- **Explicit leave:** marked `left`, lobby continues at 3. The remaining members use **Queue
  again**; there is no backfill.
- **Completion:** any member presses **Good squad**, which closes the lobby for them and records
  their vote. **Three of four presses counts the group as a successful radshare** (see
  *Successful radshare*). Absence of a press is not evidence of anything.
- **Re-queue:** **Queue again** performs a normal `queue.join` with a fresh `enqueuedAt`. There
  is no group re-queue — it would need per-member consent and could not re-queue disconnected
  members without contradicting *Presence*.
- **A no-show is handled by leaving and re-queueing, and nothing more.** The remaining members
  leave the lobby and queue again themselves. No automatic backfill, no preserved position, no
  code-based rescue. This is deliberate: with no room scarcity and first-four matching, a fresh
  `enqueuedAt` costs only the people who joined that bucket in the meantime, which is usually
  nobody.
- **Share code: group formation, not backfill.** A **6-character alphanumeric code** (`K7M2QP`)
  lets friends group up *before* queueing. Two or three people join the same group by code, queue
  as a unit, and the matcher fills the remaining seats with randoms. This is the partial-group
  case from the original design — queue as one, two or three and get filled to four — and it is
  the thing Discord LFG handles worst.
  - **It is not a recovery mechanism for a no-show**, and the design must not claim it is. Using
    a code to replace someone who vanished would mean already knowing a fourth person who holds
    that relic and is free right now, which is exactly who the queue exists to find.
  - **It has a matcher requirement** (deferred along with the feature): a group of two or three
    must land in the same match or none of them do. The current matcher takes the first four by
    `enqueuedAt` with no concept of a group, so group entries are a real addition to
    `planMatch`, not a UI feature.
  - **The code carries no information about the lobby.** An earlier draft used
    `RAD-LITHG2-6F3A`, which leaked the relic and left roughly four hex digits of real
    randomness. Six alphanumeric characters give `36^6` ≈ 2.18 billion combinations (≈1.07
    billion if the generator drops the confusable `I`, `L`, `O`, `U`, recommended because those
    are the characters people mistype when reading a code off a screen). With a few hundred live lobbies and a
    two-hour maximum lifetime, guessing into a stranger's squad stops being worth attempting.
  - **Capacity is enforced by the database, not by application logic.** "Read the member count,
    then insert" is a race: two people entering the same code for a 3/4 lobby both read three
    and both insert, producing a five-person squad that cannot open a relic together. The fifth
    insert must fail at the database — a partial unique index on `(lobby_id, slot)` or an
    equivalent transactional check — and the constraint violation is translated into the
    friendly "This squad is already full" rather than surfacing raw.
  - Code attempts are rate-limited per IP. Unknown code: "No squad with that code." Dissolved:
    "This lobby has closed."
- **Dissolution:** all members left, or 2 hours after creation, whichever is first. Closing ends
  chat, but the Good squad vote stays open for its full window.
- **Reconnect is derived from the authenticated account** —
  `SELECT … FROM lobby_members WHERE account_id = ? AND lobby_open` — so a second device, a
  private window, or cleared site data all still work. The `localStorage` token is an
  optimization for the anonymous-fast-path only, never the sole key.

### User journey and emotional arc

The target user, per premise 9: someone who wants a vaulted relic and has already failed to fill
it in recruiting chat.

| # | User does | User feels | Plan specifies |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lands on the global board, having never seen it | Skeptical. Every Warframe LFG board they have tried was empty. | Board browsable with no account — global mode is the proof surface. Activity band with real counts. |
| 2 | Signs in and finds their relic | Focused — they arrived knowing exactly what they want | Still the global board, so signing in never empties the screen. Free-text relic filter in the composer. Zero-match state names the query back. |
| 3 | Signs in to queue | Mild friction, first real cost | Clerk hosted sign-in: email, Discord or Xbox. Board already browsed, so they know what they are signing up for before they pay the cost. |

| 5 | **Waits. On a rare relic, for an unknown but longer time.** | **The danger zone. "Is anyone else even here?"** | The personal board shows their own bucket at `1/4` with a live count. | 6 | Match fires while they are elsewhere | Surprise, then payoff | Audible ping, `document.title` flip. *Match transition* above: the row they watched becomes the lobby header. |

| 10 | Presses Good squad, or never comes back | No obligation | One optional button, no scoring of individuals, nothing owed. Three of four presses marks the group successful; fewer means nothing was recorded, not that anyone was blamed. |

**Time horizons.** *Five seconds:* the board is populated and honest, or it is empty and says so
warmly — either way it is not pretending. *Five minutes:* they are queued, minimized, and the app
has given them a reason to believe waiting is worth it. *Five years:* the reason to come back is
that the long tail is the only place this works and nothing else serves it — which is why the
personal queued board is load-bearing rather than decorative.

**The weakest link is step 5**, and every mitigation in it is a real number rather than a
reassurance. If step 5 fails, nothing downstream ever runs.

### The ready gate

Between the queue pop and the lobby. A bucket reaching four does **not** create a lobby — it
opens a ready check, and the lobby only exists once all four have confirmed.

```
  bucket hits 4
       |
       v
  ready.check  --> all four get a popup: relic, refinement,
       |            four members, 60s countdown, one button
       |
       +--- all 4 confirm within 60s ---> lobby created, host designated
       |                                  (oldest enqueuedAt)
       |
       +--- 60s elapses, n < 4 -------->  ready_gate_failed
                                          |
                                          +-- did not confirm: removed
                                          |   from the queue entirely
                                          |
                                          +-- did confirm: returned to
                                              their buckets with their
                                              ORIGINAL enqueuedAt
```

**This is why there is no reputation system.** A ready check is enforcement that acts in 60
seconds, applies itself, needs no threshold, and works with four users as well as with forty
thousand. A score needs volume before it means anything, and needed a rolling window, a distinct
rater floor and an escape path to avoid becoming an unappealable ban. The gate does the job that
mattered — keeping absent people out of squads — and does it immediately.

It also removes the failure the design previously had no answer for: a no-show can no longer cost
three other people a dead lobby, because the lobby never gets created. They are back in the queue
within a minute, holding the position they already had.

**Confirmers keep their original `enqueuedAt`** when returned. They did nothing wrong, and with
no room scarcity the cost is only whoever joined that bucket in the meantime — usually nobody.
This is for honesty rather than competitive advantage.

**Non-confirmers are removed from the queue entirely** — every bucket, not just the one that
popped. Someone who missed a 60-second countdown carrying a gong, a title flip and a browser
notification is not at their machine, and leaving them queued means the next three people hit the
same dead end.

This is a deliberate choice about whose time the design protects. The cost lands on the absent
user as **one click**, because the screen states plainly what happened and the composer retains
their selection. The cost of the lenient alternative lands on strangers who did nothing wrong,
repeatedly.

The failure screen must say so in as many words — something like "You didn't confirm in time, so
you've been removed from the queue" with the selection still loaded and a single button to
re-queue. Silently emptying someone's queue would be the worst version of this.

### Match transition

The emotional peak, and it now lands on the ready check rather than on the lobby. The user is
very likely not looking at this tab when it fires — they are in Warframe, on another monitor, or
away.

**The attention-getter is a gong.** Long decay, acoustic, and deliberately unlike Warframe's own
synthetic audio palette, so nobody reacts to it mid-mission thinking the game made it. It also
carries further through game audio than a short UI blip, and its decay covers the
"did-I-hear-that" moment without needing a repeat. Muteable with a volume control in settings —
an unmuteable sound is how a tool gets closed permanently. Alongside it: the `document.title`
flip and a browser Notification where permission exists.

The rest of this section is what they see when they turn back.

On `ready.check` the bucket row the user was watching fills its last segment, lifts out of the
board, and becomes the header of the ready-check popup. Ease-out, ~400ms, from an element that
was already visible. When all four confirm, the popup gives way to the lobby and that same header
carries across — so the relic is continuously visible from board row to lobby, never re-stated.

This is deliberate, not decoration. It answers "which of my twenty buckets fired?" with no copy
at all, because the header *is* the row. It is also the **one authored motion moment** the design
rules permit, spent on the moment that earns it rather than on section entrances.

**Degradation is mandatory, because the common case is that the origin row was never on screen.**
If the tab was backgrounded, the page was scrolled away from the row, or the user had navigated
elsewhere, there is nothing to lift from: the popup renders directly with no motion. Same for
`prefers-reduced-motion`. The transition is an enhancement on a path that must work without it.

Within one second of looking, the screen answers three things: it happened, **which relic** won,
and **that a countdown is running and they have to press something**. Who hosts is answered a
moment later, in the lobby, because until all four confirm there may be no lobby at all.

### Successful radshare

**There is no reputation system.** No thumbs, no per-user score, no aggregate beside anyone's
name, no exclusion threshold. An earlier design had all of it — binary thumbs, distinct-
counterparty scoring, a rolling exclusion window — and it was cut because it does nothing until
there is enough volume for a score to mean something, and because the ready gate enforces
attendance immediately without needing any.

What replaces it is a single group-level measurement:

- **One button in the lobby: Good squad.** Optional, unattributed, and it rates the *group*, not
  the people in it. Nobody can be marked down.
- **Three of four presses marks the radshare successful**, recorded as a `radshare_successful`
  event. Two or fewer records nothing — it does not mark the group as bad, because a group where
  people simply left without pressing anything is indistinguishable from one that went wrong.
- **Nothing reads it.** No UI surfaces it, nothing branches on it, no user sees their own or
  anyone else's. It exists so that the one outcome that matters — four people actually opening a
  relic together — is a number you have when you want it.
- Pressing it also closes the lobby for that member, so it is the same action as being finished.

The threshold is three rather than four because a squad that worked will routinely have one
member who alt-tabbed into Warframe and never came back to the browser, which is the documented
happy path.

### Activity signal

A dedicated full-width band between the composer and the board. Both numbers are defined
precisely, because this is the one place the "never seed fake presence" principle is checkable:

- **"Players queued now"** — distinct accounts holding at least one queue entry. Not sockets,
  not anonymous board browsers, both of which would inflate it. Labelled "queued," not "online."
- **"Lobbies formed in the last hour"** —
  `COUNT(*) FROM lobbies WHERE created_at > now() - interval '1 hour'`.

When the user is the only member of their bucket, the row carries an inline reassurance and an
invite-a-friend affordance rather than a bare `1/4`.

**First-visit strip.** Above the board, on first visit only: four one-line steps — pick your
relics, queue, get matched to four, one person invites the rest. Dismissed once, gone forever,
stored per browser. There is no `HOW IT WORKS` page and no header link; the mockups show one and
it does not exist. The audience already knows what a relic is — the only thing they might not
know is that this board is a queue they can join rather than a list they are reading.

### Identity

**Clerk, hosted sign-in pages, verified server-side with `@clerk/hono`.** The user is redirected
to Clerk's hosted sign-in and returns with a session; the server verifies it locally against
cached JWKS on SSR routes and on the WebSocket handshake.

The split matters and drove the choice. `@clerk/hono` is an **official** Clerk SDK, and the
server is where auth does the work here. SvelteKit support is **community-maintained**
(`clerk-sveltekit`, `svelte-clerk`), so hosted pages keep that package almost entirely out of
the auth path — a redirect out, a redirect back, and a session the server checks. Embedding
Clerk's Svelte components would put an unofficial package on the critical path for a UI gain
that does not justify it.

What this removes from the slice: an email provider, DNS records, SPF/DKIM, deliverability and
bounce handling — the classic silently-failing, discovered-late problem. What it adds: email,
**Discord** and **Microsoft/Xbox** sign-in with no plumbing each, which is three of the four
providers originally wanted.

**Steam is deferred.** Steam sign-in is OpenID 2.0, not OAuth2 — Steam never implemented OAuth2,
so Clerk cannot broker it and the verification would be hand-written. It also returns only a
steamID64 and a persona name, neither of which is the Warframe IGN, so it verifies nothing that
matters here, and it covers PC-Steam only while the lobby carries a platform column for PS, Xbox
and Switch.

Accepted costs, stated rather than discovered: identity becomes a vendor dependency, and if Clerk
is unavailable new sign-ins fail. Existing sessions keep working, because JWT verification is
local.

IGN self-declared, not validated. The board is browsable
**without an account** — anonymous visitors poll the cached board and never hold a socket — and
auth is required to queue, because a queue entry is account-keyed.

### Wire protocol

`packages/protocol` defines every message.

**Anonymous clients are not on the socket at all.** `GET /api/board` returns the top-60 list plus
`hiddenCount` with a 10-second cache; the client polls it every 10s and the server renders the
same payload into first paint.

Queue side (socket, authenticated accounts only) — client→server `queue.join`, `queue.leave`;
server→client `board.snapshot`, `board.delta`, `match.found`, `error { code }`. A socket is
subscribed to the global stream while unqueued and to the account's own buckets while queued;
`queue.join` and `queue.leave` swap the subscription.

**`ErrorCode` is a closed union in `packages/protocol`, with its user-facing copy beside it.**
The server can only emit a listed code; the client's handler must be exhaustive or the build
fails; the wording lives in exactly one place rather than drifting between the two ends. The
message carries `code` alone — the string is looked up, never sent, so a copy edit is a
one-line change that cannot desynchronise. Current members: `QUEUE_CAP_EXCEEDED`,
`CODE_NOT_FOUND`, `LOBBY_FULL`, `LOBBY_CLOSED`, `MATCH_FAILED`, `READY_GATE_FAILED`,
`RATE_LIMITED`, `CHAT_SEND_FAILED`, `AUTH_FAILED`.

Ready gate and lobby (socket, authenticated accounts only) — client→server `ready.confirm`,
`lobby.rejoin`, `lobby.join_by_code`, `chat.send`, `lobby.good_squad`; server→client
`ready.check`, `ready.state`, `ready.failed`, `lobby.state`, `chat.message`, `chat.history`,
`lobby.member_changed`, `lobby.closed`.

Two shapes matter:

- **`queue.join` is an idempotent set operation** carrying the user's *full current selection*.
  The server diffs it against held entries, preserves `enqueuedAt` for unchanged buckets,
  assigns fresh timestamps only to newly added ones, and rejects the whole message if the set
  exceeds 20 pairs. This makes toggling a relic useless as a way to game rule 3's tiebreak, and
  makes `queue.leave` unambiguous: it clears everything.
- **`board.snapshot` carries a `you` field** — `{ buckets: [{bucketKey, count}] }` — sent on
  socket open, which now only happens when you are queued. Without this a reconnect cannot know which mode to
  render: queue entries are account-keyed and survive a socket swap, so after a refresh, a
  second tab, or a phone opened alongside the desktop, a new socket arrives with live queue entries while the
  client has no idea and renders the global board. Re-sending `queue.join` from `localStorage`
  would fix the display and silently reset `enqueuedAt`, costing the user their place in line on
  every refresh. One field on the snapshot makes every connect path correct by construction.
- **Deltas are coalesced into one flush per 100ms tick.** A single action causes many bucket
  changes at once — queueing 20 relics is 20 changes, and a match evicts four people from every
  bucket they occupied, which can be dozens. Unbatched, each becomes its own broadcast to every
  connected client. The server buffers changes and emits one `board.delta` per tick per client.
  The wire shape is unchanged because it already carries an array. A bucket that goes 2 → 3 → 2
  inside one tick emits nothing at all, which cuts both traffic and visual noise. The cost is up
  to 100ms of added latency on a count moving, which is imperceptible on a queue board.
- **`board.delta` carries `{ bucketKey, count, inBoard }[]`, plus `hiddenCount` on the global
  stream.** `inBoard: false` means the bucket left the top 60 and the row should be removed;
  without it the client renders a stale row forever. On the own-buckets stream `inBoard` is always
  true and `hiddenCount` is absent, because every delta there concerns a bucket you are in.

### Visual direction

**Token values live in `DESIGN.md` at the repo root.** It is the source of truth for colour,
typography, spacing, depth, browser surfaces, the component vocabulary, accessibility rules and
voice. It was seeded from the approved mockup and hand-corrected; three extracted values were
rejected and the reasons are recorded in it, because those are the ones most likely to creep
back. In particular: the extractor proposed `Arial` for every type role, and a general-purpose
gold highlight that would have collided with the Axi tier badge.

Two collisions DESIGN.md resolves that this document had not noticed, both from tier colours
sharing a hue with the accent:

- **`--tier-neo` moved from teal to light blue**, back when the accent was teal.
- **`--tier-axi` moved from saturated gold to a pale sand** once the accent became amber. Axi's
  canonical gold and amber are the same hue; an Axi badge beside a filled button would have read
  as a second button.
- **Section panels are cut.** The mockups wrap each section in a bordered box; that is chrome
  imitating structure. Sections are separated by space and a label. Only the bucket row and the
  relic chip are card-like, and both are interactive.

Dark, dense, terminal-like. **Amber primary accent (`#E0A83C`)**, taken from the approved
regenerated lobby mockup; tier colours reserved for tier badges only. Keep the Radiant explainer
card, the AGE column, the "not affiliated with Digital Extremes" disclaimer, and the
platform/crossplay row. No Discord in the chrome. The board mockups render a `4/4` row and label
buckets "lobbies"; both are wrong per the invariants above.

**Dark is a use-scene decision, not a category default.** The user is at night, on a second
monitor, beside a dark fullscreen game. That is why the surface is near-black — write it down so
nobody later "modernizes" it to light on the grounds that light is cleaner.

**No glow. Emphasis comes from structure.** Every mockup uses a coloured halo to mark the primary
button, the large activity numerals and the highlighted row. Halos are cut. Emphasis is a
hairline border, a raised background tint, and type weight — nothing else. Where real depth is
needed it is an **offset shadow with soft blur**, never a zero-offset coloured halo, which is
decoration rather than depth. Near-black plus one neon accent plus glowing edges is a stock
machine-made look, and this audience looks at game UI all day. The palette stays; the effect goes.

### Responsive

**Phone support is deferred out of the first slice. The slice is desktop-only.** The last mile
is typing whispers into Warframe on a PC, so desktop is where the product has to work first.
Everything below is the specification for when phone support lands, not v1 scope.

**Every screen works at every viewport.** One design, one lobby layout.

A phone is simply another client. It is not a companion to the desktop and carries no special
role: the two share an account and nothing else, and neither affects the other's connection. The
real phone scenario is the phone as the *only* radshare client — propped beside the keyboard,
site open, Warframe on the PC — with the user reading names off it and typing the whispers by
hand.

**Exactly two things change below 601px.** Copy-whisper buttons are absent, because a phone's
clipboard cannot reach Warframe, which runs on the PC — the user reads and types. And lobby chat
collapses behind a toggle, which the IA already names as the one cuttable element. Nothing else
is hidden.

**Two mobile-only mechanisms ship with phone support:**

- **Wake lock while queued**, per *Presence*. Without it the screen sleeps in well under a
  minute, iOS suspends the app, and the queue entry is gone.
- **An iOS foreground warning at the point of queueing**, worded to promise only what exists:
  *"Keep this tab open — iOS drops you from the queue if you switch apps or lock the screen."*
  It must not promise background queueing. Adding the site to the Home Screen is the planned
  route to that, because iOS Web Push is available only to Home Screen web apps and never to a
  Safari tab — but push is useless until a queue entry can outlive its socket, which is a change
  to premise 2 and is not designed. Until both exist, the warning says "keep this tab open" and
  nothing more.

| | Phone (≤600px) | Tablet (601–1024px) | Desktop (≥1025px) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Board** | One row per line, wrapped to two: relic + tier + refinement above, fill segments + count + age below. | Single column at full width, one line per row, AGE column dropped. | Full table, all columns. |

### Naming rule

A *bucket* is pre-match. A *lobby* is post-match. They must never share a word in the UI or code.

## Open Questions

1. **The ready-gate timeout and the Good-squad threshold are guesses.** 60 seconds to confirm,
   three of four to mark a radshare successful. Both are plausible and neither is measured.
2. **Chat moderation and reporting.** v1 ships stranger-to-stranger chat with no report button
   and no moderation. A known gap, not an oversight.
3. **Name collision with radshare.xyz.** Same word, different TLD, overlapping product. It will
   cost search traffic and cause confusion. A deliberate decision, not a discovery.
4. **Identity abuse floor.** Clerk raises the cost of a throwaway account above a bare email
   form, but a determined actor still rotates them. With reputation removed there is no score to
   farm, so the remaining abuse is nuisance rather than reputational: repeatedly joining queues
   and failing the ready gate, which wastes other people's 60 seconds. The gate limits the blast
   radius by design — nobody reaches a lobby — and nothing currently escalates against a repeat
   offender. Acceptable for now; revisit if it actually happens.
5. **Privacy policy and terms.** Required before collecting emails. Not drafted.
6. **Can a rare-relic bucket reach four inside a live socket's lifetime?** The one premise 2
   rests on. If four holders of a given vaulted relic do not appear within a session-length
   window, hard presence cannot serve the case the product exists for, and the answer would be
   to aggregate intent over days rather than minutes. This is a demand question, not an
   architecture one, and nothing gates on it — the slice's own `match_fired` and
   `ready_gate_failed` events answer it from real traffic once there is any.

## Success Criteria

- A **returning, signed-in** user goes from landing page to three IGNs in under 30 seconds, given
  three others queued for the same relic, and all four confirm at the ready gate. First-time
  users pay a sign-in round trip; excluded deliberately.
- The board never displays a bucket whose members disconnected more than 30 seconds ago, and
  that figure is the complete worst case rather than one of two stacked delays. A clean close is
  reflected within milliseconds; only a silent death costs the full window.
- The board never displays `4/4`, and never displays `0/4`.
- **A queued user always sees live counts for their own buckets**, regardless of board rank.
- **Minimizing the tab for 10 minutes does not remove you from the queue** on desktop. A clean
  tab close evicts immediately; an abnormal disconnect holds your entry, and your position in
  line, for 60 seconds.
- A user who refreshes while in a lobby rejoins it with chat history intact, on any device they
  are signed in on.
- A user whose bucket is emptied by another bucket firing sees the count change without an error.
- The empty state reads as "early" rather than "abandoned" to someone who has never seen the app.
  A judgement call; test it on a real person.
- `matches_formed` and `median_time_to_match` are queryable from day one, from `match_fired`
  events joined against `lobby_members.enqueued_at` / `matched_at`.

## Distribution Plan

Web service at radshare.io on a **small VPS** (Hetzner or similar, roughly €4–6/month), not a
managed platform. Fly was the earlier choice and was reversed: this design disables everything a
platform like Fly is good at. No autoscaling, no scale-to-zero, no multi-region, one pinned
always-on machine with a local disk — which is a description of a VPS. Paying a platform to
emulate one box is the same mistake as choosing Postgres for a single-writer workload. Fly also
has no free tier for accounts created after October 2024; the realistic floor there was $7–10 a
month for the pinned configuration this design requires.

The single-process constraint stops being something you configure and start fighting for. There
is no `auto_stop_machines` to disable and no rolling deploy that could fork the SQLite file,
because there is exactly one box and one process by construction.

**The stack on the box:**

- **Caddy** in front: automatic TLS via Let's Encrypt, reverse-proxying to the Bun process.
  WebSocket upgrades pass through natively in Caddy v2 with no special configuration.
- **systemd** unit for the Bun process with `Restart=always`, so a crash comes back without
  anyone watching.
- **SQLite** on the local disk, with **litestream** replicating to object storage. The restore
  path must be tested once rather than read about — that is the cost of choosing SQLite and it
  should be paid up front, not discovered.

**Caddy is a reverse proxy, so the socket peer is `127.0.0.1`.** The per-IP connection cap must
read `X-Forwarded-For`, never the peer address. Key it on the peer and every visitor counts as
localhost, which means the sixth connection locks out the entire userbase. This is the same
failure that `Fly-Client-IP` would have produced; changing host changed its name, not its
existence. Unlike the Fly version it is standard, documented, and verifiable locally.

**Deploys are manual-promote, not auto-on-merge** — build, ship the artifact, restart the unit.
A deploy evicts every queued user: invisible, and it hits everyone at once, which is nothing like
closing a tab. Lobbies survive, because they hold no in-memory state beyond the socket registry
and the SQLite file is on disk rather than in the deployed artifact. Mitigations:

- The client caches the user's **relic selections** in `localStorage` and auto-re-queues on
  reconnect with jittered backoff (0–5s) to avoid a thundering herd. This caches a *form
  selection*, not queue membership — the server never holds an entry for a disconnected user, so
  premise 2 is intact.
- The AudioContext is unlocked on the **first user gesture anywhere on the page** and the unlocked
  context is reused across reconnects, so a silently re-queued user still gets the match ping.
  Fallback is the `document.title` flip plus a Notification when the context is suspended.
- Deploy during low traffic; announce in-app when lobbies are live.

Relic data is vendored at build time, so a Warframe update requires a redeploy — schedule with
the same care. **Vault status deserves its own attention**: it is the single field premise 9
depends on, and it is the most volatile in the WFCD data. A Prime Resurgence rotation silently
relabels exactly the relics this product exists for, so treat a vault rotation as a deploy
trigger rather than waiting for the next feature release.

Launch channel is r/Warframe, the forums, and Warframe community Discords.

**The launch artifact is a short clip of the flow, not a board screenshot.** Pick a relic, hit
queue, the ready check pops, confirm, three names appear. That sequence shows the product working
without depending on anyone else being online when you record it — and on day one nobody will be.
A board screenshot at launch would be a wall of `1/4` rows arguing against the product.

The ready check is the right thing to lead with for a second reason: a queue pop with a
confirmation button is instantly legible to anyone who plays multiplayer games and needs no
explanation to this audience.

**Never seeded fake presence** — not in the clip, not on the board, not in the activity counts.
A clip of the real flow with one real person queueing is honest; a mocked-up busy board is not,
and one person noticing would end the project.

The board being server-rendered still matters: a visitor clicking through from the post sees
whatever is really there before JavaScript runs, and the link preview is not blank.

## Next Steps

1. **Protocol and data model.** `packages/protocol` message types; SQLite schema —
   `accounts`, `igns`, `lobbies`, `lobby_members` (with `enqueued_at` and `matched_at` copied
   from the in-memory entry at fire time), `chat_messages`, and a single
   `events(id, type, payload jsonb, created_at)` log indexed on `(type, created_at)`.
   `match_fired`, `ready_gate_failed` and `radshare_successful` are event types, not tables —
   all append-only, none read in a hot path, and future event types cost no migration.
   There is no `ratings` table; reputation does not exist in this product.
   `radshare_successful` records a lobby where at least three of four pressed **Good squad**.
   It is captured for telemetry only: nothing reads it, no success criterion depends on it, and
   no UI surfaces it. It is the one signal that a radshare actually happened in-game rather than
   four names appearing on a screen, so it is worth having before anyone needs it.
   `ready_gate_failed` records a queue pop that did not reach four confirmations.
   Plus the `Bucket` structure with FIFO ordering, created on first join and deleted on last
   removal. **Unit-test the matching contract before any UI exists** — simultaneous fire,
   tiebreak determinism, bucket creation and deletion, and the zero-`await` invariant.
2. **Auth.** Clerk hosted sign-in verified with `@clerk/hono`, self-declared IGN. Ahead of
   matching because buckets and lobby membership are account-keyed. Steam deferred.
3. **The global board, both transports.** Anonymous: cached `GET /api/board` on a 10s cache,
   polled every 10s, and server-rendered into first paint. Signed in: the same board live over
   the socket. Fixtures exist for development behind a `?dev=1` flag only — the **public** board
   is real or it is empty. This is the landing page.
4. **Queue composer.** Relic multi-select with type-ahead chips, refinement multi-select
   defaulting to Radiant, a live "n/20 buckets" indicator, one primary QUEUE button. Vault state
   and rarity carried through from the vendored WFCD data so the composer can surface them when
   a user is choosing what to queue for.
5. **Live queue state.** Connect/disconnect with `idleTimeout: 30` (Bun pings ~15s; no sweep, no
   `lastPongAt`), eviction on the account's last close, and the two `board.delta` streams (global
   while unqueued, own-buckets while queued, switching on `queue.join` / `queue.leave`).
6. **Matching and the lobby.** Fire at four per the contract; lobby with three IGNs, per-IGN
   copy-whisper, persisted chat, Good squad, Queue again, and the lifecycle rules.
7. **The Good squad button.** One optional press per member, unattributed, closing the lobby for
   them. Three of four records a `radshare_successful` event. Nothing reads it.
8. **The two board modes and the two transports.** Content follows queue state (global vs own
   buckets, 120ms cross-fade); transport follows auth (anonymous polls the cached
   `GET /api/board` every 10s and gets it server-rendered into first paint, signed-in gets both
   modes live over the socket with a subscription swap on `queue.join` / `queue.leave`). This is what makes the product serve
   premise 9 rather than just the common-relic case.
9. **Activity signal and empty state.** The band, the inline "you're first" message for a
   lone-member bucket, the invite affordance.
10. **Re-engagement.** Gong on match (AudioContext unlocked on first gesture, muteable in settings), `document.title` flip, Notification where permitted.
11. **Lobby reconnect.** Account-derived, with the `localStorage` token as an optimization.
